The science

Superposition and coherence

When two waves meet, the resultant displacement is the sum of the individual displacements (the principle of superposition). Where crests meet crests the waves reinforce, and where crests meet troughs they cancel. That is interference.

A steady, visible pattern needs coherent sources. Coherent means the same frequency (so the same wavelength) and a constant phase difference. The phase difference does not have to be zero, it just has to stay fixed. The amplitudes do not need to match, but if they differ the minima will not be perfectly dark.

Path difference and phase difference

If the sources emit in phase, the phase difference at a point depends only on how much further one wave has travelled:

phase difference = 2πΔ / λ   (or 360° × Δ / λ)

  • Δ = nλ (n = 0, 1, 2 …): waves arrive in phase, so constructive interference and a maximum.
  • Δ = (n + ½)λ: waves arrive in antiphase, so destructive interference and a minimum.
  • Anywhere else the result is somewhere in between.

In the simulation the dark lines running out from the sources are lines of destructive interference. Move P along one and the path difference stays at the same half-integer number of wavelengths. Increase d and more lines appear, because the largest possible path difference is d itself, so the number of maxima on each side is limited by d / λ.

Phasors

The small diagram shows each wave as an arrow. Its length is the amplitude and its angle is the phase at P. Adding the arrows tip to tail gives the resultant. For two equal waves with phase difference φ the resultant amplitude is 2A|cos(φ/2)|, and since intensity is proportional to amplitude squared, the intensity swings between 0 and 4I₀. The average is 2I₀, so energy is redistributed rather than created or destroyed.

Link to Young's double slit

The two slits act as the two coherent sources. On a distant screen the two paths are almost parallel, so Δ = d sin θ, and for small angles that becomes Δ ≈ dx / D. Setting Δ = nλ gives the fringe spacing w = λD / d.

Common exam slips

  • Coherent does not mean "in phase". It means a constant phase difference.
  • Always say "path difference of a whole number of wavelengths" for maxima, and "an odd number of half wavelengths" for minima.
  • Interference redistributes energy, it does not destroy it.

The science

Diffraction

Diffraction is the spreading of a wave as it passes through a gap or around an edge. It is most noticeable when the gap is about the same size as the wavelength. A gap much wider than λ barely spreads the wave at all, which is why you do not see light bending round doorways but you do hear sound do it.

Huygens' model explains it. Every point on a wavefront acts as a source of secondary wavelets, all in phase. A slit lets through only a short row of these sources, and the sideways spread is no longer cancelled by neighbours.

Single slit

Treat the slit as many tiny sources. In the direction where the path difference between the top edge and the bottom edge is exactly λ, every source in the top half has a partner in the bottom half that is half a wavelength out of step, so everything cancels. That gives the first minimum:

a sin θ = λ   and more generally a sin θ = nλ for n = 1, 2, 3 …

  • The central maximum is about twice as wide as the others and much brighter. On a screen distance D away its full width is roughly 2λD / a.
  • A narrower slit gives a wider pattern. A longer wavelength also gives a wider pattern.
  • Note there is no n = 0 minimum, because θ = 0 is the centre of the bright maximum.

Diffraction grating

A grating has a huge number of equally spaced slits. If the path difference between light from neighbouring slits is a whole number of wavelengths, every slit's light arrives in phase and you get a bright maximum:

d sin θ = nλ   where d = 1 / (lines per metre)

  • n = 0 is straight ahead, n = ±1 are the first orders, and so on.
  • Since sin θ cannot exceed 1, the highest order is the largest whole number below d / λ.
  • More slits lit means sharper, brighter maxima. With N slits there are N − 1 minima between neighbouring maxima, and the peak width shrinks roughly as 1 / N. Try the N slider.
  • Compared with a double slit the maxima are far narrower, so wavelengths can be measured much more precisely.

In the simulation the slits are a fixed 1 µm wide, so the single-slit envelope is very broad and fades the higher orders slowly.

White light

Each wavelength has its own θ for a given order, so white light is split. The zeroth order stays white because every wavelength has zero path difference there. In each higher order the spectrum runs from violet (shorter λ, smaller angle) to red (longer λ, larger angle). Higher orders are wider and eventually overlap, for example red in the second order overlaps violet in the third.

Worked example

A grating has 600 lines per mm and is lit with 590 nm light. Then d = 1/600 000 m = 1.67 µm. First order: sin θ = 590 nm / 1667 nm = 0.354, so θ = 20.7°. The highest order is below d / λ = 2.8, so n = 2 is the highest.

Adjacent nodes (or antinodes) are λ / 2 apart, so λ = 2 × node spacing.

The science

Microwaves

Microwaves are electromagnetic waves. School microwave sets typically emit at around 10 GHz, giving a wavelength of about 3 cm, which is long enough to measure with a ruler. Use c = fλ, so λ = 3.00 × 10⁸ / f.

Why a standing wave forms

The wave hits the metal plate and reflects back along the same line. The incident and reflected waves have the same frequency and amplitude and travel in opposite directions. Because they come from one source they are coherent, so they superpose to give a stationary (standing) wave.

The electric field must be zero at a metal surface, so there is a node at the plate. Reflection there introduces a phase change of π (half a cycle).

Nodes and antinodes

  • Nodes are points of permanently zero amplitude. Antinodes are points of maximum amplitude.
  • At distance u from the plate the amplitude is proportional to |sin(2πu / λ)|, so nodes are at u = nλ / 2 and antinodes are at odd multiples of λ / 4.
  • Adjacent nodes are λ / 2 apart. Adjacent antinodes are also λ / 2 apart, and a node sits exactly midway between two antinodes.

Standing wave compared with a progressive wave

  • No energy is transferred along a standing wave, because the two waves carry equal energy in opposite directions.
  • Amplitude varies with position, from zero at nodes to a maximum at antinodes. In a progressive wave every point reaches the same amplitude.
  • All points between two adjacent nodes oscillate in phase, and points in neighbouring loops are in antiphase. In a progressive wave the phase changes steadily along the wave.

The experiment

Move the receiver along the line between the plate and the emitter and watch the meter. It rises and falls, with minima at the nodes. Measure the distance across as many nodes as you can, divide by the number of gaps to get the node spacing, and then:

λ = 2 × node spacing   and then   f = c / λ

Measuring across, say, ten gaps rather than one reduces the percentage uncertainty in the spacing, because the same ruler uncertainty is spread over a much bigger distance.

As interference

At the probe the direct wave and the reflected wave differ in path by 2u. Adding the half-cycle phase change on reflection, destructive interference (a node) happens when 2u = nλ, and constructive interference (an antinode) when 2u = (n + ½)λ. That matches nodes at nλ / 2.

The science

The set-up

Light from a single source (or a laser) reaches two narrow, closely spaced slits. Each slit diffracts the light so that it spreads out, and the two spreading beams overlap on the screen. Because both slits are lit by the same wavefront, the light leaving them is coherent: same wavelength and a constant phase difference.

Why there are fringes

At a point on the screen the two waves have travelled different distances. Where the path difference is a whole number of wavelengths they arrive in phase and add to give a bright fringe. Where it is an odd number of half wavelengths they arrive in antiphase and cancel to give a dark fringe. The derivation tab shows how this gives:

w = λD / d   or   λ = wd / D

This needs D much larger than d, so that the two paths are nearly parallel and the angle is small.

What changes the pattern

  • Longer wavelength: wider fringes (red fringes are wider than blue).
  • Larger slit separation d: narrower fringes, and the pattern is more crowded.
  • Larger screen distance D: wider fringes, because the same angle gives a bigger distance.

White light

Each wavelength makes its own set of fringes with its own spacing. At the centre the path difference is zero for every colour, so the central fringe is white. Either side the colours drift apart, with blue nearest the centre and red furthest out, and further out the overlapping colours blur towards white.

Measuring w accurately

  • Measure across many fringes and divide, which reduces the percentage uncertainty in w.
  • Count carefully. Measuring centre to centre across n fringe spacings needs n gaps. Check whether a diagram's measuring arrow runs centre to centre or edge to edge before you divide.
  • Use a metre rule for D, and a travelling microscope or a ruler on the screen for w. Slit separation d is usually given.
  • Laser safety: never look into the beam, avoid reflective objects near it, and use a low-power laser (below 1 mW).

Step through the derivation of λ = wd / D. Not to scale: d and θ are exaggerated.

Notes on the derivation

  • Two assumptions carry the whole result: D is much bigger than d (so the rays are nearly parallel), and θ is small (so sin θ ≈ tan θ).
  • Here x is measured from the centre of the pattern to the nth bright fringe. The fringe spacing w is the distance between neighbouring bright fringes, which is why the n cancels.
  • For dark fringes the condition changes to path difference = (n + ½)λ, but the spacing between neighbouring dark fringes is the same w.
  • The equation only holds for monochromatic light. With white light each colour has its own w.
  • In the exam, state the two approximations if you are asked to derive or justify the equation.

The science

What a progressive wave does

A progressive wave transfers energy from place to place without transferring matter. Each particle of the medium (or each point in the field) oscillates about a fixed position, and passes the disturbance on to its neighbour a moment later.

Transverse and longitudinal

  • Transverse: the oscillations are at right angles to the direction the wave travels. Examples are light and all electromagnetic waves, waves on a rope and ripples on water.
  • Longitudinal: the oscillations are parallel to the direction of travel. Sound is the standard example. It forms compressions (particles close together) and rarefactions (particles spread out).

Quantities

  • Displacement: distance of a point from its equilibrium position, in a stated direction.
  • Amplitude A: the maximum displacement.
  • Wavelength λ: the distance between two adjacent points in phase, for example crest to crest, or compression to compression.
  • Period T: the time for one complete oscillation of a point. Frequency f = 1 / T.
  • Wave speed: v = fλ.

The wave in the simulation is y = A sin(kx − ωt), where k = 2π / λ and ω = 2πf.

Two graphs, two measurements

A displacement-distance graph is a snapshot of the whole wave, so you read the wavelength from it. A displacement-time graph follows one point, so you read the period from it. Exam questions often test whether you can tell which graph you have been given.

Phase and phase difference

Phase describes where a point is in its oscillation cycle. Points a whole number of wavelengths apart are in phase. Points an odd number of half wavelengths apart are in antiphase. In general:

phase difference = 2π Δx / λ   (or 360° × Δx / λ)

Move P and Q apart with the slider and watch the two traces on the time graph slide relative to each other.

Intensity

The intensity of a wave is proportional to the square of its amplitude, so doubling the amplitude quadruples the intensity.

The science

How a standing wave forms

A wave travelling along a string (or air column) reflects at the end and comes back. The incident and reflected waves have the same frequency and amplitude, and they travel in opposite directions, so they superpose. At certain frequencies the result is a pattern that does not travel along the string: a stationary (standing) wave.

Nodes and antinodes

  • Nodes are points of zero amplitude. The two waves are always in antiphase there.
  • Antinodes are points of maximum amplitude. The two waves are always in phase there.
  • Adjacent nodes are λ / 2 apart, adjacent antinodes are λ / 2 apart, and a node is midway between two antinodes.

Harmonics

Only certain wavelengths fit, because the ends must be nodes or antinodes. The lowest frequency is the first harmonic (the fundamental).

  • String fixed at both ends: a node at each end, so L = nλ / 2 and f = nv / 2L for n = 1, 2, 3 …
  • Open pipe: an antinode at each end, so L = nλ / 2 and f = nv / 2L, the same series.
  • Pipe closed at one end: a node at the closed end and an antinode at the open end, so L = nλ / 4 and f = nv / 4L for odd n only (1, 3, 5 …).

For a stretched string the wave speed is v = √(T / μ), where T is the tension and μ is the mass per unit length. The first harmonic is therefore f = (1 / 2L)√(T / μ). Shortening the string, tightening it, or using a lighter string all raise the pitch.

Standing wave compared with progressive wave

  • A standing wave transfers no net energy along it, because the two waves carry equal energy in opposite directions. A progressive wave transfers energy.
  • Amplitude in a standing wave varies from zero at nodes to a maximum at antinodes. In a progressive wave every point has the same amplitude.
  • All points between two adjacent nodes oscillate in phase, and points in neighbouring loops are in antiphase. In a progressive wave the phase changes steadily along the wave.
  • Wavelength in a standing wave is twice the distance between adjacent nodes.

Notes on the simulation

The oscillation is slowed down so you can see it. For the pipes, the curve shows the displacement of the air, with a displacement node at a closed end and a displacement antinode at an open end.

The science

Plane polarised light

Light is a transverse wave, and its electric field oscillates at right angles to the direction of travel. In unpolarised light the oscillation direction changes randomly and takes every direction in the plane. In plane polarised light the oscillations are in one direction only.

Only transverse waves can be polarised. Sound and other longitudinal waves cannot, so being able to polarise light is evidence that light is transverse.

Polarisers and Malus's law

A polarising filter only transmits the component of the electric field along its transmission axis. Unpolarised light passing through one polariser emerges plane polarised with half the intensity. If polarised light then meets a second polariser (the analyser) at angle θ to the first, the amplitude transmitted is A cos θ. Intensity is proportional to amplitude squared, so:

I = I₀ cos²θ

  • θ = 0°: all the light passes.
  • θ = 90° (crossed polarisers): no light passes.
  • Rotating the analyser through 360° gives two maxima and two minima.

The middle polariser

Tick the box and put a middle polariser at 45° between two crossed polarisers. Light now gets through, even though it could not before. The middle polariser rotates the polarisation direction, so each stage has a component along the next axis. With the middle one at 45° the final intensity is I₀ / 2 × cos²45° × cos²45° = I₀ / 8.

Other ways to polarise light

  • Reflection: light reflected from a non-metallic surface such as water or glass is partly polarised, and completely polarised at the Brewster angle, where tan θ = n.
  • Scattering in the atmosphere partly polarises skylight.

Uses

  • Polarising sunglasses reduce glare, because reflected glare is largely horizontally polarised and the lenses block it.
  • LCD screens use polarisers and liquid crystals that rotate the polarisation direction to switch pixels on and off.
  • Stress analysis: some transparent plastics rotate polarised light differently where they are strained, which shows up as coloured patterns.
  • Aligning TV and radio aerials with the transmitted signal, since broadcast radio waves are polarised.

Microwave demonstration

A metal grille between a microwave transmitter and receiver acts like a polariser. When the grille wires are parallel to the electric field, the wave is absorbed, and when they are perpendicular it passes. Turning the grille through 90° switches the signal on and off.
